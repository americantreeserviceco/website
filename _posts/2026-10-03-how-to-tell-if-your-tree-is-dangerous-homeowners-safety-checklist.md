Trees rarely announce a problem with one unmistakable warning. A new crack, a hanging limb, or a patch of lifted soil can matter more when it appears alongside other changes, especially after a storm or nearby construction. Use this checklist to notice concerns from a safe distance. It is not a diagnosis, and a single sign does not automatically mean a tree must be removed.

## Start with immediate safety

If a tree is moving, partly uprooted, blocking an exit, or likely to fall onto people or a building, keep everyone out of the area and contact emergency services or a qualified tree professional. If a tree or branch touches or threatens power lines, stay well away and call the utility. Never approach, climb, cut, or move a tree that may be in contact with a line.

## Walk the checklist from a safe place

Look from the ground and outside the tree’s likely fall area. Do not walk beneath hanging limbs or climb to get a closer look.

* **A new or worsening lean:** Has the trunk recently shifted, or does the ground around the base look lifted, cracked, or newly mounded? A gradual lean may be normal for that tree; a sudden change or disturbed roots needs prompt attention.
* **Cracks or splits:** Look for fresh openings in the trunk or where large branches join it. Long cracks, separated stems, or a split that appears to be widening can signal a structural problem.
* **Changes at the base:** Soil heaving, exposed or broken roots, a cavity near the root flare, or soft-looking wood can be warning signs. Keep your distance; don’t probe the trunk or dig around roots.
* **Dead or hanging limbs:** Note large dead branches, broken limbs caught in the canopy, or branches hanging over a roof, driveway, sidewalk, or play area. A small twig falling is different from a heavy limb suspended above a regular-use space.
* **Canopy decline:** Compare the tree with previous seasons. Sparse leaves, branches that fail to leaf out, or dieback spreading through the crown are worth documenting, particularly when several areas are affected.
* **Fungal growth or insect activity:** Mushrooms or shelf-like growth on the trunk or near the roots, unusual holes, or sawdust-like material can point to decay or pests. These signs need context; they do not by themselves prove the tree is unstable.
* **Recent site disturbance:** Construction, trenching, grade changes, heavy equipment, or soil compaction near the roots can affect stability and health, sometimes before symptoms are obvious.

## Consider what the tree could hit

Risk depends on both the tree’s condition and what is within reach if a part fails. A questionable limb over an empty back corner is different from the same limb over a bedroom, entrance, parking area, or public path. Note nearby targets and keep people away from any area that appears unsafe while you arrange an assessment.

## What homeowners should do next

* **Record what changed.** From a safe location, take photos and note when you first noticed the issue and whether it followed a storm, excavation, or other site change.
* **Limit access.** Keep children, pets, and visitors away from the area beneath a suspicious limb or beside a tree with fresh movement or cracking.
* **Arrange a professional assessment.** Contact a qualified arborist, preferably an ISA Certified Arborist, and describe the specific signs you observed. Ask what needs immediate attention and what can be monitored.
* **Avoid do-it-yourself hazard work.** Don’t climb, use a ladder or chainsaw, pull on a hanging branch, or try to straighten a leaning tree. Tree work near structures and power lines can be especially dangerous.

## A quick reminder

No checklist can confirm that a tree is safe, and some serious defects are difficult to see from the ground. Likewise, a lean, cavity, or mushroom does not automatically mean removal is the only answer. If you notice a sudden change or a large defect near a place people use, stay clear and get an on-site evaluation. Early advice can help you choose a safer next step for your property and your trees.
