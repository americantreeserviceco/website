Early Snow on the Front Range: Why Leafy Trees Break and What to Do After the Storm

Every few years, the Front Range gets a heavy, wet snow while maples, elms, ash and cottonwoods are still holding most of their leaves. Those leaves catch snow like open hands. A limb that easily handles a dry January snowfall can split under the weight of an October storm, and homeowners from Golden to Denver wake up to branches across driveways, fences and roofs.

Here is why early-season snow is so hard on trees, what you can safely do during the storm, and what to look for once it melts.

Why fall snow does more damage than winter snow

  • Leaves multiply the load. Deciduous trees that haven't dropped their leaves hold far more snow than bare branches, and wet fall snow is heavy.
  • Weak structure gets exposed. Branches with tight, V-shaped unions, long horizontal limbs and trees that were topped in the past tend to fail first.
  • Some species are more brittle. Siberian elm, silver maple, cottonwood and Callery pear are common in Front Range neighborhoods and are known for breaking under snow and wind.
  • Trees are still active. Wood hasn't fully hardened for winter, and soft, saturated soil can let an already-leaning tree shift.

What you can do during the storm

1. Stay out from under loaded trees. Branches can drop without warning, especially when temperatures rise and snow starts to slide. 2. Gently clear small trees and shrubs. Use a broom to push snow up and off lower branches you can reach from the ground. Don't shake trunks or strike limbs; frozen wood snaps easily. 3. Leave ice alone. If branches are coated in ice, let it melt naturally. 4. Keep away from power lines. If a tree or limb is touching a line, stay well back and call your utility. Never try to clear it yourself.

What to check after the snow melts

Walk your property from a safe distance and look for:

  • Hangers: broken limbs caught in the canopy. These can fall days or weeks later and are one of the most common causes of injury after a storm.
  • Split or cracked unions: fresh cracks where two trunks or large limbs meet.
  • Torn bark and jagged stubs: these heal poorly and invite decay unless they're cleaned up with proper pruning cuts.
  • New lean or lifted soil: a change at the base of the tree after a storm needs prompt attention.
  • Damage near targets: limbs over the roof, driveway, play areas or the sidewalk deserve a closer look first.

Don't let storm damage turn into a bad pruning job

After a storm, it's tempting to hire whoever knocks on the door first or to cut every damaged branch back to a stub. Topping and stub cuts leave trees weaker and more likely to break in the next storm. A broken limb should be assessed and pruned with a proper cut when appropriate. Trees compartmentalize wounds; pruning does not make an injury disappear. Read more in [Pruning vs. Topping](/blog/pruning-vs-topping-why-bad-tree-cutting-can-destro.html).

Get ahead of the next storm

The best time to deal with weak limbs is before the snow arrives. Structural pruning reduces long, heavy limbs and removes deadwood so your trees can carry snow loads safely.
